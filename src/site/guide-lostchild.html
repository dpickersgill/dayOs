Lost child procedure for ski areas.

What to do in the first five minutes and the next thirty, how to reunite and verify the adult, what to record, and a checklist for running a drill twice a season.

A child separated from their class or family is the call every ski area hopes stays rare. When it comes, the first minutes matter most, and nobody should be working out who does what. This procedure is a starting point. Write your own from it, train it, and drill it.

This is a template. Have your insurer and attorney review it before adopting.

Local law enforcement. Call local law enforcement as your area's own written policy says, at the time that policy sets. Decide in writing whether some cases, such as a very young child or any sign of abduction, mean calling at once. This guide does not set that time; your policy, your insurer and your attorney do.

The first 5 minutes

The first person to know a child is missing starts the procedure. Nobody waits to be sure.

  1. Call it. Any staff member who learns a child is missing calls it in at once: the instructor, the desk, a lift operator, a parent at the ticket window. Use one plain phrase on the radio that everyone knows.
  2. Capture the description. First name, age, height, jacket and helmet colors, pants, skis or snowboard, the last place and time seen, who they were with, and their ability. Ask whether they have a phone and whether there is a usual meeting place.
  3. Notify patrol. Patrol dispatch runs the search from here. Give dispatch the description, the last place seen and the radio channel.
  4. Notify lift operators. Every lift operator watches the load and unload for the description and holds the child at the lift shack if seen. Top stations watch who is getting off.
  5. Watch the base area and parking lots. Put staff at the base area exits, the lodge doors and the parking lot entrances. Children head for the car, the lodge, the rental shop and the last lift they rode.
  6. Keep the adult in one place. The parent or the instructor stays at a set point, usually the ski school desk or the patrol room, with a phone, so the child can be returned to them and so they can answer questions.

The next 30 minutes

  1. One person leads. The patrol director or the person your policy names runs the search, and everyone reports to them.
  2. Search by area. Assign areas: the class's last run and lift, the terrain park, the tree areas near the beginner terrain, the base area, the lodge restrooms, the rental shop and the parking lots. Record who searched each area and when.
  3. Use what the area already knows. The last lift scan of the child's ticket, the last class check-in and where the class last reported help narrow it down.
  4. Keep the family informed at set times, even when there is nothing new.
  5. Widen the search on the clock your policy sets, and contact local law enforcement when your policy says to.

The reunion: verifying the adult

When the child is found, bring them to the set point, not to whoever happens to be nearby.

  • Release a child in a lesson only to an adult on the booking's pickup list, after checking photo ID. Follow your pickup and release policy.
  • For a child who was with their family, confirm the adult with the child, and check ID if anything is unclear.
  • Ask whether the child is hurt, cold or upset. Patrol checks them if there is any doubt.
  • Call off the search by radio, with the time, so every searcher hears it.

The after-action record

Write the record the same day, while people remember. It shows what happened and what to change. Record:

  1. The timeline: when the child was last seen, when it was called, when patrol and lifts were told, when the base and lots were covered, when the child was found, and when the search was called off.
  2. The description given, as it was given.
  3. Who searched where, and when.
  4. Where and by whom the child was found, and their condition.
  5. Who the child was released to, who released them, and the ID check.
  6. Whether law enforcement was contacted, when and by whom, under your policy.
  7. What to change: radio calls, gaps in coverage, a meeting place that confused children.

Keep it as a safety record. It is not a patient record unless the child needed care, in which case patrol writes its own incident report.

Running a lost child drill twice a season

A procedure nobody has practiced is a sheet of paper. Run a drill twice a season: once early, when staff are new, and once mid-season, on a busy day if your managers will allow it. Use a staff member as the "child" with a written description, and time each step.

Lost child drill checklist

  • Date, time and who ran the drill written at the top of the record
  • The call went out on the radio within one minute of the report, with the agreed phrase
  • The description captured in full: first name, age, colors, equipment, last place and time seen
  • Patrol dispatch received it and named who leads the search
  • Every lift operator confirmed by radio that they are watching
  • Base area exits, lodge doors and parking lots covered, with the time each was covered
  • The adult kept at the set point with a phone
  • Search areas assigned and recorded, each with who and when
  • The decision on law enforcement made at the time your policy sets, and recorded
  • The reunion at the set point, with the adult verified against the pickup list and photo ID
  • Search called off by radio, and every searcher confirmed
  • Times written down for each step, compared with your targets
  • Debrief held the same day, with what to change and who changes it

How Verdynt/OS helps when a child is lost

In Verdynt/OS the ski school and ski patrol share one call:

  • Lost child button. On the instructor's My day or the desk's Pickup screen, Lost child asks for jacket and helmet colors (families can give them ahead on their lesson page) and opens a high-priority patrol dispatch call.
  • What patrol sees. The call carries the child's first name and age, the colors, the class, the instructor, the meeting place, and the last check-in, lift scan or class location the school reported.
  • The photo, only while it matters. Patrol sees the child's photo only while the search is open or its dispatch call is still active. After that, patrol cannot open it.
  • The school stays informed. The director, supervisors and the desk get a phone alert, and the school sees the call's status in a red strip.
  • Found closes it. Found records who found the child, where and when, adds a note to the dispatch call and closes it when patrol has no case on it.

Patrol's dispatch rules stay as they are, and no patient record is made for a lost child who needs no care.

Common questions

Who should call a lost child at a ski area?

Anyone who learns of it, at once. The instructor, the desk, a lift operator or a ticket seller should all know the radio phrase and the first steps, without waiting for a supervisor.

When should a ski area call the police about a lost child?

When your area's own written policy says to, at the time it sets. Decide in writing which cases, such as any sign of abduction, mean calling at once, and have your insurer and attorney review that part of the policy.

What description should staff take?

First name, age, height, jacket and helmet colors, pants, skis or snowboard, the last place and time seen, who the child was with and their ability, plus whether they carry a phone.

Where do lost children usually go?

Often to somewhere familiar: the car, the lodge, the rental shop, the last lift they rode or the lesson meeting place. Cover the base area exits and parking lots in the first five minutes.

How often should we run a lost child drill?

Twice a season: once early, when staff are new, and once mid-season. Time each step, write it down and change the procedure where it was slow.

Who can take the child home after they are found?

For a child in a lesson, only an adult on the booking's pickup list, after a photo ID check, as your pickup and release policy says.
