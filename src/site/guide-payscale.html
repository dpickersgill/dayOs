Instructors talk about pay on the chairlift. If two people with the same certification earn different rates and nobody can say why, you will hear about it, and some of them will not come back next season. A published pay scale answers the question before it is asked: here is what each level earns, here is what moves you up.

This guide gives you the structure, not the numbers. Your rates depend on your market, your lesson prices and your budget, and on the wage rules where you operate.

The structure of a ski instructor pay scale

Most ski school pay scales are built from the same parts. Start with the base, then add what your school values.

Base rate by certification level

The base rate is what an instructor earns for an hour of group teaching at their level. Certification is the usual measure because it is earned outside your school and can be checked.

  • PSIA-AASI (Professional Ski Instructors of America and the American Association of Snowboard Instructors) certifies instructors in the US at Levels 1, 2 and 3.
  • CSIA (Canadian Ski Instructors' Alliance) certifies ski instructors in Canada, and CASI (Canadian Association of Snowboard Instructors) certifies snowboard instructors, each with four levels.
  • Not yet certified. Many schools hire new instructors who certify during their first season. Give them a row on the ladder too.

If you hire from more than one body, decide how their levels map onto your ladder and write it down. Do not assume that the same level number means the same thing in each.

Steps for credentials

Specialist credentials such as children's specialist, freestyle and adaptive take training and assessment, and they make an instructor more useful for certain lessons. Two common ways to pay for them:

  • A step up the base rate for every lesson, whatever the lesson is.
  • An add-on only on lessons that use it: the children's specialist add-on on kids lessons, adaptive on adaptive lessons, freestyle on park lessons. This rewards the credential where it earns its keep.

Seniority

Returning instructors cost less to train and they keep families coming back. A small step for each few seasons taught (for example, after 3 seasons and after 6) rewards that. Count seasons the same way for everyone, and write down how a gap season counts.

Private lesson and request premiums

  • Private lessons sell for more than group lessons. Many schools pay a higher private rate per level, so instructors want to teach them.
  • Requests. When a guest asks for an instructor by name, that instructor brought the business back. A request premium per lesson says so. Decide whether it applies to group lessons, privates or both.
  • Semi-private lessons sit between the two. Give them their own rate or treat them as privates; write down which.

Training pay

Clinics, certification prep and staff training before the season all take instructor time. Set a training rate per hour, decide which sessions are paid, and log who attended. Ask your payroll provider or attorney which training must be paid where you operate.

How to handle tips

Tips are the instructor's, not part of the pay scale. Keep them out of the rate, pass them through in full, and keep them out of any comparison of instructor cost against lesson revenue. Ask your payroll provider or accountant how tips are reported where you operate.

Keeping the pay scale fair and transparent

  • Publish the ladder. Put the whole ladder in the staff handbook and on the staff room wall: every level, every step, every premium. Secret rates are where the chairlift talk starts.
  • Say what moves someone up. A new certification, a new credential, a season taught. Say when the change takes effect: the next pay period, or the date the certificate was issued.
  • Let instructors see their own pay lines. Each lesson, its rate and any add-on, before payroll is approved. An instructor who can see a missing private lesson on Tuesday can tell you before the check is wrong.
  • Answer questions in writing. When an instructor questions a line, answer it where they will see it, and fix it in the same period if it is wrong.
  • Review it once a year. Before hiring opens, check the ladder against your lesson prices and the rates your instructors tell you about. Change it once, announce it, and keep the old ladder on file.
  • Exceptions are written down. If one instructor is paid off the ladder, record why and who approved it.

How Verdynt/OS applies your pay scale

In the ski school app, the ladder you build here becomes the rates payroll uses:

  • Pay ladder. Each instructor has a certification level (PSIA-AASI 1, 2, 3 and Trainer/Examiner; CSIA 1 to 4; CASI 1 to 4) and specialist credentials (Children's Specialist 1 and 2, Freestyle, Adaptive). The ladder sets a rate per level for group, semi-private and private lessons, hourly work and program meetings, and an add-on per credential, per lesson and per hour.
  • Add-ons where they fit. Children's Specialist is paid on lessons for under-18s, Adaptive on adaptive lessons and Freestyle on park and freestyle lessons.
  • Own rates for exceptions. An instructor's own rate wins over the ladder, which is how a seniority step or any other agreed rate is set. Only owners and managers change the ladder, and a school director cannot change their own level or credentials.
  • Rate warnings. Instructors who would be paid nothing for a kind of lesson the school pays are listed on the director's Today board and on Payroll, with a link to set the rate.
  • Training pay. The director logs training sessions and who came. Each attendee gets a training line at their hourly rate on My pay and in payroll.
  • My pay with Flag. Instructors see their own pay lines for the open pay period, and whether it is approved. Flag on any line tells the director what is wrong; the director answers on Payroll, and the answer shows under the line.

Owner reports count instructor cost from the pay lines, with tips left out, so lesson revenue against instructor cost compares like with like.

Common questions

How are ski instructors usually paid?

Most schools pay an hourly rate set by certification level, with higher rates for private lessons and extra for specialist credentials, requests and seniority. Some pay per lesson instead. Whichever you choose, publish it.

Should certification level set an instructor's pay?

It is the most common base because it is earned outside the school and can be checked. Add steps for what your school also values, such as children's or adaptive credentials and seasons taught.

Do instructors get paid for training?

Many schools pay a training rate for clinics and preseason training. Ask your payroll provider or attorney which training must be paid where you operate, and log who attended each session.

Are tips part of an instructor's pay rate?

No. Tips belong to the instructor. Keep them out of the rate and pass them through in full. Ask your payroll provider or accountant how they are reported.

Should we publish the instructor pay scale?

Yes. A published ladder, and each instructor seeing their own pay lines before payroll is approved, settle most pay questions before they become complaints.

Why does this guide not list rates?

Rates depend on your market, your lesson prices and the wage rules where you operate. The worksheet leaves every cell blank for your own numbers.

This guide is general information, not legal, tax or payroll advice. Check wage and tip rules where you operate with your payroll provider or attorney.
